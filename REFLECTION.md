# Reflection

*Remnants of a New World* — Zeid Al-Ruzouq

The human experience of life exists beyond vision alone; most define the full range of human senses as having five categories: sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch. These senses permit us to experience our environment and pursue meaning. They make us what we are. However, I believe that there is more to the experience of life than just these five senses. A sixth sense? Or even a spectrum of senses? The way I think of it is that, at least with our traditional system of five senses, all senses derive, in essence, from the fundamental interactions of the forces of gravity, electromagnetism, and the strong and weak nuclear forces. Whether it be sugar molecules attaching to our taste receptors or our retinas producing and sending electrical signals to our brains after being bombarded by light photons, these senses ultimately emerge from the same fundamental interactions. So why don’t we just define them as one sense, or have a continuous spectrum of senses, if they are all fundamentally one and the same? Rather than treating these senses as entirely isolated categories, I wanted my project to similarly blur the boundaries between different ways of experiencing and interpreting something.

In this project, I aim to give readers a way to make their own sense, draw their own paths, and become authors of their own found memories. In the design of my online portfolio, I set out to imitate free-form video games that use content I “found” (more on that later) as the material, while also incorporating a broader range of human senses rather than relying on vision and text alone.

You start at a search screen, where you are already given something to search for. However, you notice that you can remove phrases like “Remnants of a” to get only the phrase “New World,” which, when searched, takes you immediately to the end, but you can also search for anything or press “I’m feeling lucky” to start on a random level. When you first enter, strange but calm music fills the atmosphere; you are in an ordinary room. Scattered notes line the wall. Gloomy but warm colors fill the room. A lamp illuminates a note that you start reading. While the atmosphere is calm, you still feel a sense of urgency. A sense of curiosity? Primal instinct? Your “curiosity” drives you to understand and find meaning in the memories that lie in front of you. The poem talks of transactions and presence in medicine? There is a map at the lower end, and you learn of an “End?” Are you in a maze? You make your way through a strange landscape. A portrait of an unknown figure awaits behind “alien” notes. You remove it to reveal a figure who stares at you, as if he knows you, but you sure don’t. You come across more notes, then are suddenly greeted by a computer terminal. But weren’t you just in an office? You click “run,” but “it does not work” — strange? You come across more notes and notice that each level is an obstacle leading to more memories, but do the levels themselves hold memories? You begin to notice a change in the tone of the poem; was there more than one person here? Outside, things have changed too. It gets darker and starts raining. You continue, but your path is interrupted by an “error”; you resolve it and continue. The path stops; there is a rainbow cat. You click and move it horizontally across the page, avoiding meteors, but not the poems. Suddenly, you stop, and you stumble across a divergence. Should I continue forward, or take “the path not taken?” You choose to continue on your usual path and come across a black hole. It sucks in the memories you were looking for; you try to save them, but accidentally click too many times on the black hole and are returned to the search page. You take the same journey again, following the map, but this time you choose the other path and reach another level — a break. While taking a break and repairing another bug, you ask yourself if the path you took this time left a memory behind. Your train of thought is interrupted when the sun’s glaring beams catch you off guard; the rain has stopped, and the plants have grown and overtaken your shelter. However, it feels calming, like an end to a search for meaning or the beginning of something new. You finish the break and continue. You finally reach the end? You find a list of “achievements.” You know you have missed something. These formal choices—the shifting colors, sound, scattered layout, environmental changes, and nonlinear paths—intentionally complicate the content by requiring readers to search, and create in the process, for meaning rather than encounter the poems in a fixed order.

The experience just described is one of various scenarios a typical reader could follow (some of which I included in the notes spread throughout the portfolio). The online portfolio mixes my found poems, weavings, and generated text so that they are in conversation with one another. Its “strangeness” is derived from the fundamental differences and areas of slippage between the analogue and digital processes I use to create media. In analogue processes such as weaving, I can manipulate and place meaning exactly as I see it, which allows me to communicate a message to readers more directly. However, I am limited by both the arduous labor involved and the medium’s inability to incorporate the full range of senses. On the other hand, digital processes like my PowerPoint found poems and the Markov generator allow me to incorporate a much wider sensory range through media such as sound, touch, and sight, which, by connecting to readers differently, as Ron Padgett puts it in “Creative Reading,” introduces “misunderstandings” that can create something newfound. A natural consequence, though, of the digital processes that I use is that legitimate meaning becomes harder to deliberately incorporate, as algorithms such as RiTa.js’s markov() function are devoid of contextual memory. An example is the part of the generated poem that reads:

> “In medicine, my values change from hand to hand  
> For they cannot remain theory alone”

This was generated from my application for a pre-med program, in which I wrote about why medicine matters to me and whether I had contemplated another career path at any point in my life. In the essay, I write that I do not want my values to change and that I fear my humanity will fade if I become a doctor due to repeated exposure to procedures, reducing medicine to a transaction to be completed rather than focusing on the person. However, the generated text inverts my meaning in a way that claims I plan to change my values to accommodate a more human side, as if I am already a doctor. This misunderstanding continues throughout the generated poem, where it also makes it seem as though I am lecturing Dr. Kalanithi on how to become more human, even though my original intent was the opposite: I sought Kalanithi’s experiences to teach me how to maintain my own humanity. This parallels what Kim describes when she writes, “Of the removed found. Of the found that does not live. Of found devoid of memory,” suggesting a “found” that has been separated from the lived memory that originally gave it meaning. In this sense, the algorithm can retain the language while forgetting what lies behind it: it holds no purpose of its own for reading or writing and avoids the responsibility for properly carrying forward the intended meaning of the original text. This is where the Markov chain’s memorylessness becomes most visible in my project: it can “remember” nearby words and patterns while forgetting the larger history that gave those words meaning.

Moreover, the project’s methodology complicates who I can name as the author. For context, the online portfolio was created with the assistance of Cursor, an AI-powered IDE that provides access to agents from parent LLMs such as Claude and ChatGPT to provide code completions. From my perspective, I created the weaving and its message, one of the sources of the text, the choices and organization of words selected from the Markov generator, and the ideas and features that my prompts asked Cursor to create. Cursor responded to my prompts by using agents to create what I asked for, but based on how those agents interpreted my requests. A third contributor was Paul Kalanithi’s When Breath Becomes Air, which acted as both a second source for the Markov generator and provided context for my prompting of Cursor. However, a fourth contributor to the project was the secondary material provided to Cursor that further shaped its understanding of the project. I added examples of sites from HTML Review that inspired me to create my portfolio as an online simulation, the assignment description and requirements to explain how the website was intended to showcase my work, and even literary material such as Eunsong Kim’s “Found, Found, Found: Lived, Lived, Lived” and Ron Padgett’s “Creative Reading,” which affected my prompting by giving me ideas and context to consider in the creation of the project. Taken all at once, this project seems to serve as a community effort that incorporates voices from many sources, whether indirectly or directly, and does not seem to have a sole author.

This project remembers, forgets, and creates new meaning. It remembers the sentiments expressed in the found poems, the importance of human virtue in medicine, and the assignment’s requirements. It forgets any individual person or entity’s identity or goal: that I am a student, not a doctor; Kalanithi’s circumstances when writing his text; and the original narrative context of the individual texts used. At the same time, it creates new forms of media, such as sound and interactive touch, through which to view the remembered themes of retaining one’s humanity, as well as new ways to encounter someone else’s misunderstandings. The main takeaway, at least my own takeaway, from this project is that memory is found, revitalized, and then shared. Memory is similar to the domain of mathematics in that it does not really exist in nature as a physical object, but we use it to infer the logic we observe in our natural environment. Maybe memory has always existed, waiting to be discovered; maybe no one and everyone is original at the same time.
